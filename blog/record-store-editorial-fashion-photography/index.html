Editorial Fashion Photography Inside a Record Store

A Set Built Out of Crates, Not a Studio

Some of the best editorial backdrops are not built, they are found. For this shoot, the location was a record store: rows of vinyl, hand-lettered genre signs, and the kind of warm, cluttered lighting you cannot fake with a softbox. Two models, styled in bold color against all that texture, gave the whole set a lived-in, unposed feeling that a plain studio backdrop never quite gets you.

This is the kind of session I love shooting as an editorial and portrait photographer: real locations, real props already in the room, and a shot list loose enough to let genuine moments happen between takes. I take a similar approach at high-energy events, like the We On The Streets popup I covered in DC.

Directing Two Models Without Losing the Candid Feel

The brief for this session was simple: keep it playful, keep it moving, and do not let it look staged. With two models instead of one, the dynamic between them becomes as important as either individual look. I gave loose direction, a general area to work in and a mood to hit, and let them riff off each other. The strongest frames from the day, the two of them sharing lollipops by the drink cooler, laughing mid-pose, came from that in-between space rather than a planned setup.

Working in a real, functioning store also means working around the space instead of controlling it. Shelving, signage, and product all became compositional tools rather than obstacles. That constraint is part of what makes location-based editorial work more interesting than a clean studio cyc wall.

Shooting in Mixed, Low Retail Lighting

Record stores are rarely lit for photography. Overhead fluorescents mix with warm accent lighting and the occasional neon sign, and none of it agrees on color temperature, which is exactly the kind of mixed-source scenario Lightroom's white balance tools are built to correct selectively. Here is how I handled it.

Settings That Worked

  • ISO 800-2500 to keep shutter speed usable without flash
  • Aperture f/1.8-f/2.2 to isolate the models from the busy shelving behind them
  • Shutter speed at 1/160s minimum to keep candid movement sharp
  • RAW files only so mixed color temperatures could be corrected selectively in post

Working With What the Room Gives You

Instead of fighting the store's existing lighting, I shot toward it. Backlighting from the front windows and practical lights in frame added depth that a strobe setup would have flattened out. When the ambient light was too warm or too green, I let it read as part of the shoot's aesthetic rather than correcting it into something neutral.

Why Found Locations Beat a Blank Wall

Working with a real, lived-in space changes what a session can capture. This shoot worked because the location did half the styling for us: the crates, the signage, the warm clutter of a working store. My job was to find the angles that let that texture support the models instead of competing with them.

A studio gives you control. A real space gives you texture, color, and history you could never build from scratch.

If you have a concept built around a real place, whether it is a record store, a corner shop, or somewhere with genuine character, I want to hear about it. Those are the sessions that turn into the strongest work.

Editorial Fashion Photography FAQ

Do you need to shoot at a business, or can this happen anywhere with character?

Any real location with texture and history works. Record stores, corner shops, and other found spaces bring styling elements you cannot replicate in a studio.

Do you help coordinate permission to shoot inside a business?

Yes. I work with the business owner ahead of time to confirm hours, access, and any restrictions before the shoot date.

How many models can a session include?

One or more. Multi-model sessions add a different dynamic, since the interaction between models becomes part of the story alongside individual poses.

Do you bring lighting equipment for low-light retail spaces?

Usually no. I prefer to work with the space's existing mixed lighting, using wide apertures and higher ISO rather than flattening the mood with a strobe setup.

Can you shoot editorial content for a brand or business, not just personal work?

Yes. Editorial and styled sessions work well for brand content, lookbooks, and social campaigns in addition to personal portfolio work.

How long does an editorial session like this take?

Most run 90 minutes to 2 hours, depending on the number of models, outfit changes, and locations within the space.
