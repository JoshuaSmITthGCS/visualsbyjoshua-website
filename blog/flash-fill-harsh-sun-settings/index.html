Flash as Fill in Harsh Midday Sun: The Exact Settings I Use

How to use TTL fill flash and high-speed sync to shoot portraits at noon without blown highlights or raccoon-eye shadows, with exact compensation values.

Nobody books a session for 11am because it looks good. They book it because that is when the graduation ended, or when the venue let them in, or when everyone was free.

You still have to deliver. Here is the setup that turns noon into workable light.

Start by turning the subject away from the sun

Before you touch a flash, put the sun behind your subject. Backlight does three things at once. It rims the hair and shoulders, it stops the squint, and it puts the face into open shade so the light on skin is even.

Now the face is two to three stops darker than the background. That gap is what the flash fixes.

The base settings

MeteringSpot on the face

Expose for the background first. Set aperture and ISO for the look you want, then raise shutter speed until the sky holds detail. On a clear July day in Charleston that lands around 1/2000 at f/2.8 and ISO 100.

Then bring the flash up until the face matches.

Why minus one stop and not zero

TTL meters for a correct exposure of the whole scene, which means it will try to make the flash the main light. You do not want that. You want the sun to still look like the sun and the flash to be invisible.

Dialing flash exposure compensation to -1.0 or -1.3 EV puts the flash roughly a stop under ambient. The result reads as natural light with the shadows opened up. Nobody looks at the photo and says "flash."

On darker skin, start at -1.0 rather than -1.3. Deeper skin absorbs more light and TTL tends to underexpose it.

Why HSS is not optional

The A7III syncs with flash at 1/250. At noon, f/2.8 and ISO 100 and 1/250 gives you a completely blown sky.

High-speed sync lets the flash pulse rapidly through the entire shutter travel, so you can shoot at 1/2000 and still use flash. The cost is output. HSS burns roughly two stops of flash power, which is why a 76Ws speedlight struggles past about eight feet in direct sun and a 200Ws strobe does not.

Distance is your power dial

Flash falls off with the square of distance. Moving the light from ten feet to seven feet gives you roughly a full stop more without touching a setting.

If you are running out of power, walk closer before you raise ISO.

Outdoors, a dome on a speedlight is dead weight. There is no ceiling. Take it off.

Reading the result on the back of the camera

Three checks before you move on.

Catchlights. Look at the eyes at 100 percent. If there is no catchlight, the flash is too far or too low. Catchlights make a portrait look alive.

Shadow under the chin. A soft gradient is right. A hard black edge means the flash is too high, too close, or too strong.

The background. If the sky went grey or the leaves lost color, you lifted ambient exposure too far. Raise shutter speed, not flash power.

The fast version for graduation days

Graduations happen at whatever time the school picked and you get fifteen minutes per family. Run this.

  1. Put the sun behind the grad, campus building or trees in the background.
  2. Manual, f/2.8, ISO 100, shutter to taste for the sky.
  3. Flash on camera, TTL, -1.0 EV, HSS on.
  4. Shoot at seven to nine feet.
  5. Check catchlights on the first frame, then stop checking.

FAQ

What flash compensation should I use for fill in sunlight?

Start at -1.0 EV. Go to -1.3 EV if the fill looks obvious. Go to -0.7 EV for darker skin or when the subject is heavily backlit.

Do I need HSS for outdoor flash?

Yes, any time you want to shoot wider than about f/8 in daylight. Without it your maximum shutter is 1/250 and you will blow out the background.

Can a speedlight overpower the sun?

Not at distance and not with a modifier. It works as fill. For a subject lit brighter than the sky you need a 200Ws strobe or more.

Why do my outdoor flash photos look flat?

The flash is too strong relative to the ambient. Bring compensation down and let the sun do the shaping.

What is the best time of day for outdoor portraits?

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset. When you cannot get that, find open shade or backlight the subject and fill.

How do I stop the sky from going white?

Raise shutter speed. Aperture and ISO control the flash relationship. Shutter speed controls ambient only, which is exactly what you want when the sky is the problem.
