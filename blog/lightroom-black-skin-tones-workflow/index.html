Editing Black Skin Tones in Lightroom: A Repeatable Workflow

Use this step-by-step Lightroom workflow for accurate deep skin tones, including HSL values, masking order, and fixes for grey or orange skin.

Most editing tutorials were built around light skin. Follow them on a dark-skinned subject and you get one of three failures. Skin goes grey. Skin goes orange. Or the shadows fill with green and magenta noise the moment you lift them.

This is the order I edit in, and why each step is where it is.

Get It Right in Camera First

No workflow fixes an underexposed file. Camera meters pull toward the average brightness of the scene, and on a dark-skinned subject in a bright room that means underexposure by half a stop or more.

Fixes on set:

  • Spot meter off the cheek, not the whole scene.
  • Add a third to two thirds of a stop of exposure compensation when the background is bright.
  • Watch the histogram and put skin in the upper middle, never the left third.
  • Always shoot RAW. Recovering a JPEG on deep skin tones produces banding immediately.

For the full camera side, read Sony A7III Event Settings.

Step 1: Profile and White Balance

Start with Adobe Color or the Camera Standard profile. Skip the creative profiles until the very end.

Set white balance off something neutral in the frame, then correct by eye on skin. Deep skin tones show a color cast faster than pale skin does, and a mixed lighting venue will push them green under fluorescents or magenta under LED.

Typical starting point on Sony files under daylight: 5200K to 5600K, tint +6 to +12.

Step 2: Global Exposure and Tone

Work in this order. Each control affects the ones after it.

ControlTypical rangeWhat it does here
Exposure+0.20 to +0.60Recovers the meter's underexposure
Highlights-20 to -50Controls specular shine on foreheads and noses
Shadows+10 to +30Opens shadow detail without going flat
Whites+5 to +20Sets the white point
Blacks-5 to -15Keeps real black in the frame

Do not push Shadows past about +40. Lifting shadows aggressively on brown skin drags out color noise and shifts the midtones toward grey. If a face is too dark, fix exposure and use a mask, not the global shadow slider.

Step 3: The Tone Curve

A gentle S curve adds contrast without crushing the midtones where skin lives.

  • Lift the shadow point slightly for a soft matte base.
  • Pull the lower third down about five points.
  • Push the upper midtone up about five points.
  • Leave the top highlight untouched.

Then check the individual RGB curves. Lifting the blue channel in the shadows cools the darkest areas, which is usually where a green cast hides.

Step 4: HSL, the Part That Matters Most

This is where skin either looks right or looks like a filter.

Orange is the primary skin channel. Red carries the deeper tones and lips. Yellow is the one that ruins everything.

Starting values for deep skin:

ChannelHueSaturationLuminance
Red-2 to +4-5 to +50 to +8
Orange-4 to +5-8 to +2+5 to +15
Yellow-10 to -20-10 to -25-5 to +5

Pull yellow saturation down. Yellow is what makes brown skin look sallow or jaundiced. Dropping yellow luminance slightly also deepens the richness without adding saturation.

Raise orange luminance to bring skin up without brightening the whole frame. This is the single most effective slider for deep skin tones.

The Test That Catches Errors

Zoom to 100 percent on the cheek and check the RGB readout under the histogram. On healthy brown skin in neutral light, red should be highest, green in the middle, and blue lowest, with green and blue reasonably close. That comparison is the fastest way to catch a cast before it reaches a client gallery.

When green climbs above red, the file has a cast.

Step 5: Masking

Only now do you touch local adjustments.

  1. Subject mask, then subtract the background. Raise exposure 0.15 to 0.30 to separate the subject.
  2. Face mask. Lower Texture by 10 to 20 for smoothing that keeps pores. Never use Clarity on skin, it darkens midtones and adds a grey cast.
  3. Eyes. Raise Exposure 0.25, Whites 10, Sharpness 20. Stop there. Over-brightened eyes look uncanny.
  4. Teeth. Saturation -15, Exposure +0.15. Do not desaturate past -25 or teeth read grey.
  5. Radial mask on the background. Exposure -0.20 to draw attention forward.
  6. Highlight recovery on shine. A small brush at Highlights -30 on foreheads and nose bridges. Shine is a lighting problem, not a skin problem, so fix it lightly.

Step 6: Color Grading and Finish

Split toning belongs at the end and it should be subtle.

  • Shadows toward blue or teal at 8 to 15 saturation.
  • Highlights toward warm orange at 5 to 10 saturation.
  • Balance slightly toward highlights.

Add Texture +5 globally and sharpen at Amount 40, Radius 1.0, Detail 25, Masking 60. High masking keeps sharpening off skin and on eyes, hair, and clothing.

Keeping a Gallery Consistent

Edit one hero image completely. Copy settings and paste to the rest of the same lighting setup. Then go image by image and correct only white balance and exposure.

Do not paste HSL across different lighting conditions. Indoor tungsten and outdoor shade need different yellow values, and a gallery where skin shifts color between frames looks worse than a gallery that is slightly off but consistent.

Build a preset once you have a look that repeats. Save it as a starting point, not a finish.

The Four Mistakes

  1. Cranking Clarity. It grinds midtones and greys out brown skin. Use Texture instead.
  2. Lifting shadows to +60. Color noise and a flat, hazy face.
  3. Global saturation increases. Push Vibrance instead, or better, adjust HSL channel by channel.
  4. Heavy skin smoothing. Deep skin has visible texture and specular highlights. Removing them makes people look like plastic.

FAQ

Why does dark skin look grey in my photos?

Two causes. Underexposure in camera, or lifted shadows and raised Clarity in post. Fix exposure globally, then raise Orange luminance in HSL.

What HSL settings work for Black skin tones?

Raise Orange luminance, lower Yellow saturation, and leave Red close to neutral. Exact values depend on the light, but that direction is consistent.

Should I use skin smoothing on darker skin?

Lightly. Texture at -10 to -20 keeps pores. Aggressive smoothing removes the specular highlights that give deep skin dimension.

How do I fix a green cast on skin?

Adjust the tint slider toward magenta, then check the RGB readout on the cheek. Green above red always means a cast, no matter what the screen shows you.

Do camera profiles matter for skin tones?

Yes. Adobe Color and Camera Standard render skin differently. Pick one and use it across a gallery so tones stay consistent.

How long should editing a 50 image gallery take?

Between 90 minutes and three hours once you have a workflow and a preset. Longer than that means you are editing image by image instead of by lighting setup.
